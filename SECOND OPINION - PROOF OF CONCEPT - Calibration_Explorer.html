Do these models know when they don’t know?

Each model answered the same multiple-choice pet-health questions (options A to E, where E means “insufficient information”). For every answer we know three things: whether it was right, its token confidence1 (the probability the model put on the letter it chose) and its stated confidence (the number it gave when asked how confident it was). The gap is stated minus token confidence.

Does a clear winner mean a right answer?

We know the probability the model gave every option, not just the one it chose. The winning margin is how far its top option was ahead of the runner-up. A narrow margin means it was torn between two answers. Each square is one answer from the selected model, grouped by winning margin: right answers stack above the line and wrong ones hang below. The percentage is how often the top option was right.

How the models compare

For readers who want the technical detail. Click a row to focus the page on that model.

Calibration error
How far, on average, a model’s confidence is from how often it is actually right. If it says 80% and is right 80% of the time, the error is 0. Lower is better.
AUROC
Take one answer the model got right and one it got wrong, at random. AUROC is the chance that it was more confident about the right one. 0.5 means its confidence is no help in spotting its own mistakes, the same as tossing a coin; 1.0 means it is always more confident when right; below 0.5 means it tends to be more confident when it is wrong. Roughly, above 0.8 is useful and below 0.7 is weak. The winning-margin column scores the gap between the top two options in the same way.

1 Token confidence is read from the model’s own output probabilities. Models write one small chunk of text, called a token, at a time and give each possible chunk a probability. Here the answer is a single letter, so token confidence is the probability the model gave that letter. It never appears in the reply, so it shows how sure the model was independent of what it said.
